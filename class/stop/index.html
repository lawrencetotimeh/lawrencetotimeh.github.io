The Stop Kit

What to do before, during and after a police stop. Learn the rules, get the officer's info the right way, write it all down, and report it if your rights were crossed. Built for Mississippi, DC, Maryland and Virginia.

Five rules for any stop

Learn these now, not during the stop. During a stop, your only job is to get home safe. The fight happens after, on paper.

1

Stay calm. Keep your hands where they can see them.

Officers are trained to watch hands. Slow, visible hands lower the temperature for everybody.

Example: You're driving at night. Pull over, turn on the inside light, turn the car off, hands on top of the wheel. Before you reach for anything, say what you're doing.

"My license is in my back pocket. I'm going to reach for it now."

2

Don't run, fight or argue. Even if you think it's wrong.

You can't win an argument on the side of the road. You can win it later with a complaint, a record and witnesses.

"This is racial profiling and you know it!"

Stay quiet. Remember the words they used. Write them down after.

3

Find out if you can leave.

If you're free to go, you can calmly walk away. If you're not, you're being detained, and you can ask why.

"Officer, am I free to go?"

If they say yes: leave calmly. Don't run. If they say no: ask "What's the reason I'm being stopped?" and remember the answer word for word.

4

You can stay silent. Say it out loud.

Staying quiet is a right, but you have to use it clearly. Just going quiet can look like you're ignoring them.

"I'm choosing to remain silent. I'd like to speak to a lawyer."

Exceptions to know: If you're driving, you must hand over your license, registration and proof of insurance. And never give a fake name. In Virginia that's a crime by itself (Va. Code 19.2-82.1), and anywhere it makes things worse.

"I only had one drink." "I was just about to..." Explaining can be used against you.

Give what the law requires. Then stop talking.

5

You don't have to agree to a search.

If they ask to search you, your bag, your car or your phone, you can say no. If they search anyway, don't touch them or block them. Say it once and let the record show it.

"I don't consent to any searches."

Example: "Mind if I look in your backpack?" You: "I don't consent to searches." They search anyway. You stay still and write down later exactly what they searched and what they said.

If you're under 18

You have extra protections. Ask for them out loud.

"I'm under 18. I want my parent or guardian. I want a lawyer before I answer questions."

  • Maryland: before police question a child in custody, the child must talk to a lawyer first, and police must try to reach a parent (Child Interrogation Protection Act, 2022). Bills to weaken it failed in 2024, 2025 and 2026. Verified
  • Virginia: before questioning an arrested child, police must notify a parent and let the child contact them (Va. Code 16.1-247.1). No lawyer-first rule. Since 2025, officers questioning a child in custody may not knowingly lie about important facts. Verified
  • DC: whoever takes a child into custody must promptly notify the parent (D.C. Code 16-2311). Verified
  • Mississippi: police must keep trying to reach a parent and invite them to be there for questioning (Miss. Code 43-21-303). "Invite" means questioning can start without them, so ask for your parent out loud. Verified

Can I record?

In general, yes. You can record police doing their job in public, as long as you don't get in the way. Federal courts covering Mississippi have said so (Turner v. Driver, 2017), and so have courts covering Maryland and Virginia (Sharpe v. Winterville, 2023). DC police policy recognizes it too.

"I'm recording. I'm not interfering."

Keep it safe: hold the phone where they can see it, step back if told, and don't argue about the camera. If you're the one stopped, it's usually safer to let a passenger or friend record.

Know these by heart

Not statute numbers. Five calm lines. Said the right way, they tell an officer you know your rights without starting a fight.

  1. Am I free to go?
  2. I'm choosing to remain silent.
  3. I don't consent to any searches.
  4. I want a lawyer. I want my parent.
  5. May I have your name and badge number?
Can I record when they stop me?

Usually yes. You can record police doing their job in public. But during your own stop, safety orders come first. If an officer tells you to keep your hands where they can see them, do it.

Do: before you hit record, say what you're doing. If a passenger is with you, it's safer for them to record.

"I'm going to record. My phone is in my right hand."

What if I'm already recording before the officer starts talking?

Keep recording. Just don't let the phone become the problem. If it's on a dash mount, leave it there. If it's in your hand, keep it low and still, not in the officer's face. Tell them right away.

"Just so you know, I'm recording. My phone is on the dash. My hands are on the wheel."

If the officer tells you to put it down, follow the order and keep talking calmly. Your words still count, and their camera may still be rolling. Pro tip: put a phone mount on your dash before you ever get pulled over, so you never have to reach for anything.

Remember: you're on camera too

Many officers wear a body camera, usually on the chest. Police cars often have dash cameras. Not every officer has one: Mississippi has no statewide body camera requirement. Where there is a camera, it records the officer, and it records you.

That can protect you. If something goes wrong, you or your family can request that video later. Your calm voice and your visible hands are your proof.

  • Look for the camera on the officer's chest.
  • Speak slowly and clearly toward the officer so the camera hears you.
  • Say what you're doing before you do it.
  • Say your rights out loud, calmly, once.

"I'm keeping my hands on the wheel. I'm not resisting. I don't consent to any searches. I'm choosing to remain silent."

The camera hears everything, good and bad. Yelling, cursing or pulling away ends up on video too. Stay calm so the video tells your side. Documentation beats conversation. Their camera is documentation. Make sure it documents you doing everything right.

They told me to put my phone away. Do I have to?

Follow the order in the moment. Officers can give safety orders during a stop, and refusing can turn a ticket into an arrest. You won't win that argument on the side of the road. Your report afterward can.

"Okay. I'm putting it down. I'd like to call my parent when we're done."

Then: write down that you were told to stop recording, and when.

I want to call my parent during the stop

Ask first. Don't just grab your phone.

"Can I call my mom? I'm under 18."

If they say no, don't reach for it. Ask again calmly when the stop is over. If you're taken into custody, each of our four places has rules about reaching a parent. See "If you're under 18" on the Before tab.

Can they look through my phone?

Not without a warrant, in most cases. The Supreme Court said so in Riley v. California (2014).

"I don't consent to a search of my phone."

Don't unlock it. Don't give your passcode. If they take it anyway, don't fight. Write down who took it and when.

They told me to get out of the car

Get out. During a lawful traffic stop, officers can order the driver out (Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 1977) and the passengers too. That second case came from Maryland: Maryland v. Wilson (1997).

Do: move slowly, keep your hands visible, close the door behind you. You still don't have to consent to a search.

When do I have to show ID, and when don't I?

It depends on what you're doing when you're stopped.

  • Driving: yes. Hand over your license, registration and proof of insurance when asked.
  • Passenger: usually not required. In these four places we found no law that makes a passenger show ID just for riding in a car that got stopped. If you're told you're being detained or suspected of something, ask. Recheck
  • Walking: none of these four places has a general law that makes a person walking show ID. In DC, if you're stopped for a pedestrian traffic ticket, like crossing against the light, you must give your true name and address, but you don't have to carry or show an ID card (D.C. Code 50-2303.07). Verified If an officer is writing you a ticket or arresting you anywhere, refusing your name can lead to being taken in to be identified.
  • At school: your school's rules can require your student ID. That's a school rule, not a police stop rule.

"Am I being detained? Am I required to identify myself?"

Three rules that never change: never give a fake name or fake ID (in Virginia that's a crime by itself, Va. Code 19.2-82.1). Not having ID on you is usually not a crime unless you're driving. And showing ID is not agreeing to a search.

Example: You're walking home with two friends. An officer pulls up: "Let me see some ID." You: "Officer, am I being detained?" Officer: "No, just checking." You: "Then I'm choosing not to show ID. Am I free to go?" If yes, walk away calmly.

I'm the passenger. Do I have to give my name?

In these four places we didn't find a general law making passengers give their name. Rules can depend on the situation, so ask.

"Am I required to identify myself?"

Never give a fake name. In Virginia, that's a crime by itself.

"I'm not detained," but "I'm not free to go." What does that mean?

It means you ARE detained. The words don't decide it. What happens decides it. Under the law, if a reasonable person wouldn't feel free to leave, you've been stopped (United States v. Mendenhall, 1980; Florida v. Bostick, 1991).

"Okay. So I am being detained. What's the reason?"

Then stay put. Don't walk away. Remember both answers word for word. "Not detained" plus "not free to go" is exactly what your report and their camera should capture.

Can police lie to me?

Sometimes, yes. During questioning, police are generally allowed to use tricks or say things that aren't true (Frazier v. Cupp, 1969). They might say "Your friend already told us everything" or "This will go easier if you just talk."

Virginia is different for kids. Since 2025, Virginia officers questioning a child in custody may not knowingly lie about important facts, including with fake documents (Va. Code 16.1-247.1). Verified Maryland lawmakers have tried to pass a similar rule every year since 2022, and it hasn't passed. We found no such law in Mississippi or DC.

That's why you don't explain yourself. You can't be tricked into saying something you never say.

"I'm choosing to remain silent. I want a lawyer. I want my parent."

What can police do, and what can't they?

They can:

  • Stop you if they have a reason to suspect a crime or a traffic violation.
  • Stop you for a real traffic violation even if they're really curious about something else (Whren v. United States, 1996). That's why a broken tag light can start a stop.
  • Order you out of the car.
  • Pat down the outside of your clothes if they reasonably think you're armed (Terry v. Ohio, 1968).
  • Ask you questions. You don't have to answer.
  • Arrest you if they have probable cause.

They can't:

  • Keep you longer than the stop needs without a new reason. Once the ticket is done, the stop should be done (Rodriguez v. United States, 2015).
  • Search your phone without a warrant (Riley v. California, 2014).
  • Search your car or bag without your consent, a warrant or probable cause.
  • Force you to answer questions.
  • Use more force than the situation calls for.
  • Stop you just because of your race.
They're trying to get me out of character

Some officers may push your buttons, on purpose or not. A sarcastic question. An insult. Standing too close. Talking back usually isn't a crime by itself. But pulling away, stepping toward an officer, reaching suddenly or refusing a lawful order can give them a legal reason to detain or arrest you.

If they try to get you out of character, staying in character is how you win. You don't win the argument on the street. You win it on camera, in your report and in your complaint.

Do: breathe. Slow down. Hands visible. Short sentences. Let the camera see a calm young person who knows the law.

They said, "We just want to talk"

That's usually a voluntary conversation. You can decline.

"Am I free to go?" If yes: "I'm choosing not to answer questions. Have a good day."

Then leave calmly. Don't run.

The school resource officer wants to ask me questions

A school officer is still police. You can ask for your parent. In Maryland, if you're in custody, you must talk to a lawyer before police can question you, even at school (Cts. & Jud. Proc. 3-8A-14.2). Verified

"I want my parent here before I answer any questions."

The officer won't give me a badge number

Don't push. Read the name tag and badge on the uniform. Remember the car number and plate. Write down the time and place. That's enough to file.

I think they're wrong, and I'm being arrested anyway

Don't resist, pull away or run. Even if the arrest is wrong, resisting can become its own charge.

"I'm not resisting. I'm choosing to remain silent. I want a lawyer."

The fight happens in court and in your complaint.

Before they leave

This is how you get the officer's information without saying anything that hurts you. Calm voice. One time each. If they won't answer, don't push. Look for it instead.

  1. Officer, am I free to go?Makes it clear whether the stop is over.
  2. May I have your name and badge number, please?In Maryland, a complaint has to include the officer's name. Get it now. If they won't say it, read the name tag and badge on the uniform.
  3. Which department are you with?City police, county police, sheriff, state police and school police each have a different place to file a complaint.
  4. What was the reason for the stop?Remember the exact words. Write them down word for word later.
  5. Is there a report or incident number for this?That number is how you request the report and the body camera video later.
  6. Can I have your card?Many officers carry one. It has the info you need, printed.
  7. Where and when is my court date?Only if you got a ticket. Signing a ticket usually just means you received it, not that you agree. Sign it, then fight it in court. Refusing to sign can get you arrested in some places.

Don't say this at the end

"I'm gonna sue you." "I'm getting your badge."

Say nothing about a complaint. Just file it later.

"I know my rights!" (yelling)

Use your rights quietly, one sentence at a time.

"Look, I wasn't even speeding, I was going like 40."

Don't explain. Your side goes in the report and in court.

If they won't give it to you

  • Read the name tag and badge number on the uniform.
  • Remember the car number (often on the back or side) and the license plate.
  • Note the time and exact place. Dispatch logs can be matched to it later.
  • As soon as you're safe, open your phone's notes and type everything. Then fill out the Stop Report here.

Hold them accountable
What actually happens when you report an officer, and when you can sue.


  

    
Who works for who

    
Police officers are public employees. Your family's taxes pay their salaries. Sheriffs are elected by voters. Police chiefs are hired by mayors and county leaders who are elected by voters. Officers answer to the public, and that includes you.

    
Every department has written rules officers must follow, usually called general orders or a policy manual. Many departments post them online. Those rules sit on top of state law and the Constitution. When you know the rules they operate under, you can tell when one was broken.

    
Try it: search "[your department] general orders." The Ask Better tab builds that search for you.

  


  

    
Complaint vs. lawsuit

    

      
A complaintA lawsuit

      
        
What it isTelling the department or an oversight board an officer broke the rulesAsking a court to rule your rights were violated and order payment

        
Who can do itAnyone, including teensUsually through a lawyer. Under 18, a parent files for you.

        
CostFreeMany civil rights lawyers only get paid if you win

        
What it can lead toInvestigation, discipline, retraining, a recordMoney damages, policy changes, a public court record

      
    

  


  

    
What a complaint does

    

      
1. It starts an investigationby Internal Affairs or a civilian board.

      
2. It ends with a findingSustained (it happened and broke the rules), not sustained (not enough proof either way), unfounded (it didn't happen) or exonerated (it happened but was allowed).

      
3. Sustained can mean disciplinefrom retraining to suspension to firing.

      
4. It stays in the department's recordseven if it isn't sustained. One complaint might go nowhere. Five complaints about the same officer tell a story. Whether the public can see those records depends on the state (see the table below).

      
5. Serious misconduct can cost an officer their badgeState boards certify officers and can take that certification away, which keeps them from working as police.

      
6. It can help someone else laterIf a department was warned about an officer and did nothing, that record can matter in a future lawsuit.

    

    
Be real about it: most complaints don't end in discipline. File anyway. A record that doesn't exist can't protect the next kid.

  


  

    
Can I sue a cop?

    
Sometimes. A federal law, 42 U.S.C. 1983, lets people sue state and local officers who violate their constitutional rights.

    
    
      
  • The hard part is "qualified immunity." Courts protect officers unless they broke a right that was already "clearly established" (Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 1982). In practice that often means an earlier case with very similar facts. A lot of cases stop here.
    • 
      
  • You can sometimes sue the city or county too, if the violation came from its policy or custom, like bad training or a pattern it ignored (Monell v. Department of Social Services, 1978).
    • 
      
  • There are deadlines. Some state-law claims need a written notice to the city or county within months. Those notice rules don't apply to the federal civil rights claim (Felder v. Casey, 1988), but miss them and you can lose other claims.
    • 
      
  • Talk to a lawyer early. Your Stop Report, your complaint number and your body camera request are what a lawyer will ask for first.
